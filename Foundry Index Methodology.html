The Foundry Index

How we rank institutions and what we won't fake

The Foundry Index is our point of view on where a teenager can train for a skilled career and get an honest return. It is not a government or accreditor standard. This page shows exactly how a rank is built, from which data, so you can judge it for yourself.

The five pillars

Every ranked program is scored 0–100 — the Foundry Score. Each metric is normalized within a peer group: the programs on the same list that share its federal program code. A welding program is compared with other metalworking programs, machining included, because the federal code groups them together — not with a nursing program. The pillars are then combined using published, per-list weights. A rank is just the order of those scores within one published list, so the same program can score a little differently on two lists.

Weights differ by list — an earnings-focused list leans harder on Pillar 1. The exact profile for every published list is shown live below.

The honest-data rules

This is where we diverge from rankings that silently drop or backfill missing data.

  • Missing earnings become "Emerging," never a fake rank. Federal data withholds earnings when only a few people graduated, to protect their privacy. Small certificate programs — often exactly our audience's programs — may have no published earnings. Those programs are still listed, with an "Emerging" label, in a separate tier (up to 200 per list; see below), never interpolated above measured programs.
  • We never backfill a program's earnings with whole-school numbers and present them as the program's result. In the federal data we use, completion and net price exist only for the whole school, and we say so.
  • Earnings are one median, not a range. It's the middle figure for the program's graduates, measured 1 year after they finished (or 4 or 5 years after, when the 1-year figure isn't published). We record which, and the Foundry app and Foundry Connect's lists show it next to every earnings figure. A median is a midpoint: plenty of graduates earn less, and plenty earn more. The aviation maintenance list is the exception: there, the figure is the job's national median pay from BLS (see the sources below).
  • Small samples are disclosed, not penalized. A 22-graduate program isn't pushed down the rank for its size; it carries a confidence note.
  • Outcomes are history, not a promise. Each federal figure comes from the release that was current when the list was last built, so it describes students who finished years ago, not this year's class. Only the FAA and California board figures carry a fixed period (see the sources below). We don't show which federal release a list was built from yet, and the date in the panel above is when the list was first published, not how recent its data is.

When a pillar's data is missing for a program, the score is computed on the available pillars, re-normalized to 100% — a gap never counts as a zero — and we record exactly which pillars were measured for every score. A program is ranked only if it has an earnings figure (Pillar 1). Completion alone never earns a rank; without earnings, a program is Emerging. On the aviation maintenance list, that figure is the job's national median pay from BLS, so every school there is ranked and the other pillars decide the order.

Emerging schools — not a demotion

An Emerging school is one we couldn't score on outcomes yet — not a school we scored and ranked low. It's the honest label for a data gap, and it matters most for exactly the programs our students care about: small, new, or specialized ones the federal data hasn't caught up to.

  • Why a school lands here. To earn a Foundry Score, a program needs a published earnings figure (Pillar 1). Federal data withholds earnings when only a few people graduated, so a small certificate program often has no published earnings to score. Without earnings, the program is Emerging instead of ranked, even when its completion rate is known: completion sharpens a ranked program's score, but it can't earn a rank on its own.
  • It's a gap in public data, not a verdict on the school. A brand-new welding program with 18 graduates can be excellent and still be Emerging — the government simply hasn't published its numbers. Emerging says "we won't pretend to know," never "this school is worse."
  • We never fake a rank to fill the gap. Emerging schools are never interpolated, estimated, or slotted above measured programs. That's the whole point: a made-up rank would be more harmful than an honest "not enough data yet."
  • How it's shown. Emerging programs are still listed — with an "Emerging" label, in their own tier below the ranked list, with whatever figures we do have. On a long list, the first 200 Emerging programs show, A to Z by school; in the Foundry app, searching a school's name finds any of them. The reader sees the school exists, sees why it isn't ranked, and can still explore it.
  • Programs move out of Emerging as data arrives. When a program graduates enough students for its federal earnings to be published, it can earn a Foundry Score the next time its list is built. Emerging is a starting point, not a ceiling.
The short version: "Emerging" means we don't have the earnings data to rank this school honestly — so we say that, plainly, instead of guessing. A ranked school and an Emerging school are answering two different questions: how did its graduates do versus we can't tell you yet.

Every source, and how current it is

College Scorecard — US Dept. of Education
Program-level earnings and debt, keyed by 4-digit CIP code + credential level, plus the school's accreditor code. This is what lets us rank a welding program, not just a whole school. College Scorecard combines two years of graduates into each figure to reduce noise. The earnings count only graduates who received federal student aid and were working, not back in school, that year; the debt counts only federal loans.
Vintage: the release that was current when each list was last built. The figures describe students who finished years earlier, not this year's class.
IPEDS, via College Scorecard — US Dept. of Education
The school-wide completion rate and average net price, read from College Scorecard's institution data, which draws them from IPEDS (every school taking federal aid reports to it). Both count first-time, full-time students: completion is the share who finish within 1.5 times the normal program length, and net price is what those receiving federal aid paid after grants and scholarships. They describe the whole school, not one program.
Vintage: the release that was current when each list was last built.
Accreditation — federal College Scorecard accreditor code
For most schools, the accreditor is taken from the federal accreditor_code field (e.g. ACCSC, ABHES, NACCAS, regional). Career-training accreditors (like ACCSC or NACCAS) score highest, other national and programmatic accreditors next, regional accreditors after that. A school with no recognized accreditor on file gets no accreditation score, never a zero. The exception is the 59 FAA-certified aviation maintenance schools on our curated list: for them we record FAA Part 147 certification in place of the federal code, in the top tier, on the aviation list only. On every other list, those schools use their federal accreditor like any other school.
Vintage: current federal file.
FAA AMTS — aviation-maintenance licensure
For aviation maintenance, the licensure pass rate is the FAA's Aviation Maintenance Technician School Minimum Passage Rate report (14 CFR §147.25), covering 8/2023–12/2024: passing students ÷ (passing + failing) on FAA mechanic tests within 60 days of graduation, matched to schools by name and FSDO region. The rate is attached to each matched school's program in the broad vehicle-maintenance code (CIP 47.06), where federal data files aviation maintenance alongside automotive and diesel. It counts only on the aviation list, never on the Automotive & Vehicle Maintenance list or the Top Technical Schools for Earnings list, which share that code.
Vintage: FAA report through Q4 2024. Officially PRELIMINARY — see the caveat below.
CA Board of Barbering & Cosmetology — cosmetology licensure
For California cosmetology schools, the pass rate is the Board's "Examination Results by School" report, aggregated across license types (cosmetologist, barber, esthetician, manicurist) and weighted by test-takers. Only schools with at least 30 test-takers are included — smaller cohorts are omitted, never guessed.
Vintage: Q4 2024. California only today — see the coverage note below.
BLS OEWS — typical (median) pay for aviation maintenance
Federal earnings data can't single out aviation maintenance at a school (it's folded into a broad vehicle-maintenance code), so that list uses the national median annual pay for aircraft mechanics and service technicians (SOC 49-3011), from the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics. It's a median: half the workers in the job earn more and half earn less. It's the job's pay, not this school's graduates' pay: the same figure for every school on the list, so it shows what the career pays without separating one school from another, and debt isn't compared to it.
Vintage: the latest annual figure when the list was last built.
On the FAA aviation figures: the FAA passage-rate data is officially preliminary and is not for compliance use until January 2027. We show it because it is the best school-level licensure signal that exists for aviation maintenance. The pass rates shown in the lists don't carry that label yet, so read them with this caveat in mind.
Licensure coverage is partial — because the public data is. A licensure exam pass rate exists here only where an official source publishes it school-by-school: nationally for aviation (the FAA), and for cosmetology in California (the state board). Many states publish only statewide aggregates, not per-school results, so a by-school rate simply doesn't exist to cite for them — this coverage is limited by what the sources release, not by effort. Schools without a licensure source are scored on the pillars they do have — Pillar 4 re-normalizes over the available signals rather than penalizing a school for a gap in public data. We add a state or vocation whenever an official body publishes by-school results, and we'd rather leave a metric blank than fabricate one. By design, every licensure rate we show traces to an official government source — the FAA and the California state board — never a third-party estimate or an exam vendor.

Pillar 5 launches at 0% — and is earned in

Pillar 5 (Student Experience) is a flywheel, not a cold input: it only carries signal after Foundry has users, but the Index has to be credible before that. So it is never a launch dependency, and its weight is disclosed here as a versioned roadmap from day one.

  • Ships as a Four-Pillar Index (v1). At launch Pillar 5's weight is 0%, and it's still 0% today; Pillars 1–4 re-normalize to 100%. No reviews are collected yet. Student Experience will be introduced in a future edition, only once there are enough verified reviews — the roadmap is the transparency, not a gap we hide.
  • Phased in per school, gated by review volume. A school with no reviews is left out of the pillar, so it neither helps nor hurts. A school with only a few reviews is pulled most of the way to its peer-group average; its own reviews count for more than that average only once it has more than 20 verified reviews. The planned global weight rises 0% → 5% → 10% across annual editions as the total number of verified reviews grows. Thin data never corrupts a ranking.
  • Reviews will come from real, verified students — people who actually attended or seriously considered a program. We haven't started collecting them yet. Quality over quantity, honestly labeled: 30 verified reviews beat 3,000 anonymous ones. Pillar 5 is small by design (a planned 10% cap) and explicitly subjective.

Corrections, and what a score is not

Every Foundry Score can be traced: we record which pillars fed it and how each one scored, and programs are listed with the figures behind them, like earnings, completion and, where one exists, a licensure pass rate. Foundry Connect shows those score parts next to each program. The point is for a student to learn to read outcomes critically — not just trust a number.

Correction process

Spot an error? Anyone — a school, a student, a counselor — can flag it by emailing hello@postmarkdigital.com. Include the list, the school and program, the figure you think is wrong, and a link to the official source that shows the right one. We check it against our sources and either fix it or explain why the figure stands. Corrections are part of the process, not an exception to it.

Scores reflect historical, publicly-reported outcomes and are not a promise of any individual's result. The Foundry Index is Foundry's point of view — not a government, accreditor, or official standard, and it makes no claim of official status.
